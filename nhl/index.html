How Picks Work ▶
Every skater on the slate, priced to score at least one goal.
  • What the price is. The modelled chance he scores at least one goal, shown as American odds with the raw probability beneath. These are fair odds with no vig — a real sportsbook will always price worse. A price assumes he dresses, which is how books settle it: a scratch is void.
  • It is calibrated, not a rating. Out of sample across three seasons the number means what it says — the top tenth of the board was priced at 34.8% and scored 34.6% of the time, the bottom tenth 3.8% and 3.7%.
  • Poisson, not a coin flip. Goals per skater-game run variance/mean 1.004 — as close to Poisson as real data gets — so the model projects expected goals and the anytime price is 1 − e−expected. That is also what the NFL touchdown board is, one link function over.
  • What drives it. His career scoring — goals, shot volume and shooting percentage together — takes the model from a 0.61 ranking to 0.69 on its own. Recent ice time is next, then power-play minutes, recent shots, the opponent's goals allowed, his team's offence and home ice. Final ranking accuracy (AUC) 0.706.
  • What is deliberately absent. Recent goals (his last ten games) add nothing once recent shots are in — goals are what shots turn into by luck. Opponent shots allowed and penalty kill were tested and are nulls. A separate even-strength and power-play model, summed, was tested and is worse than one model, because power-play minutes already carry that channel.
  • It is recalibrated. A monotone (isotonic) map fitted on out-of-sample results trims the top end, where the raw model runs a little hot. It halves the calibration error without changing the order of the board.
  • Only the current roster is priced. A skater's stats are filed under the club he finished last season with, so the board reads each team's roster directly — otherwise anyone who moved this summer would be priced for his old team.
  • Cut lines. Each maximum-price filter shows the hit rate that cut actually produced out of sample — so tightening the board is a measured trade, not a guess.
  • Honest limits. Built on 182,608 skater-games across four seasons, never yet graded against a live night, and not measured against sportsbook prices — a fair number is not the same thing as an edge. No starting-goalie or line-combination data yet: those need a lineup feed the free NHL API does not have.
Commas match any of them — narrows whatever the filters already show

How Shots Works ▶
Projected shots on goal for every skater on the slate, priced against the standard lines.
  • It is a volume market, and volume is what hockey predicts well. Goals are a share market and mostly luck game to game; shots are a role. A skater's shots per game splits half-against-half at 0.82, and his ice time at 0.98 — higher than the snap share that carries the whole NFL touchdown board. That is why this is the market we built first.
  • His own shot rate carries it. Career shots per game alone takes the model most of the way — from a league-average guess to −10.8% log loss. Recent ice time is the next largest term, then his last ten games, then power-play minutes.
  • Prices are negative binomial, not Poisson. Per player, shot counts run variance/mean 1.13 against the 1.00 a Poisson assumes, so a Poisson pushes too much probability past the line. The wider distribution fixes it — the same reason the NFL receptions board is priced this way.
  • Individual Corsi and Fenwick are in the feed, were tested, and are not in the model. Total shot attempts correlate 0.93 with career shots per game and unblocked attempts 0.98. Both are worse than plain shots per game on their own, and adding Corsi back to the finished model makes it worse, not better. They measure the same thing less directly.
  • The opponent matters here, which is unusual. Shots allowed per game by the other team is the largest gain after usage — worth more than power-play minutes. On the NFL boards defence has failed this test five times; in a shot-volume market it passes.
  • Built on 182,608 skater-games across four seasons, fitted walk-forward — every season predicted by a model that never saw it. Out of sample the ladder is honest to within a point at every line.
  • Rates are on a log scale — and that fixed the stars. The first version fed shot rates in raw, which makes a projection grow exponentially with a skater's rate. Out of sample it priced MacKinnon at 5.76 shots a game when he took 4.21, Pastrnak at 4.91 against 3.65. On a log scale the projection is a power law in the rate, and the league's top 25 shooters now come in 2% under their actual volume instead of 5% over. Quoted-line calibration error fell from 1.7 points to 0.6. An isotonic correction on top was tested and made it worse, so there isn't one.

How Saves Works ▶
Projected saves for every projected starter, priced against the standard lines.
  • Saves are a shot-volume market, and the other team supplies the volume. The biggest single input is how many shots his opponent takes, season-long and over its last ten games. Then his own saves per start and how many shots his club lets through. Save % adds nothing to a saves projection — a goalie stops the shots he faces at nearly the same rate as the next one, and the volume is what moves.
  • The league keeps getting quieter, so the level floats. Saves per start fell every season we trained on, 27.3 to 24.1. A model fitted on older seasons prices newer ones low, so the projection is the league's saves per start over the last 30 days, times how this goalie, his defence and tonight's opponent compare to the rest of the slate. Opening night, before there are 30 days, uses last season's level.
  • The price is two curves, because goalies get pulled. About one start in 18 ends with the goalie pulled, and a pulled goalie keeps about half a game's saves. That gives saves a long left tail; a single curve bends the other way and makes every over near the middle look worse than it is. So the price is a full-game curve plus a small pulled-goalie one. Out of sample that puts every line from 20.5 to 32.5 within half a point of what happened.
  • Starters are projected, not confirmed. The NHL doesn't publish starters ahead of time. The board prices the goalie with most of his club's recent starts, shows how many, and Results voids a start he didn't make.
  • Built on 10,076 starts across four seasons, fitted walk-forward — every season predicted by a model that never saw it. The busiest 2% of projections still run hot: those are the nights a goalie is most likely to be pulled.

How Goals Allowed Works ▶
Projected goals against for every projected starter, priced against the standard lines.
  • Goals allowed is mostly the game total. What a goalie gives up tracks how many goals the game is expected to have far more than anything about him. Without a betting line the best inputs are team goals for and against, home ice and back-to-backs — and together they only just beat the league average.
  • So the projection leans on the average, on purpose. Fitted raw, the model's spread ran about twice too wide out of sample: the leakiest-looking spots averaged 3.1 goals against a projected 3.6. Its projections are pulled toward the league mean by the amount that held up on seasons it never saw.
  • The price is tighter than a Poisson. Starts run variance/mean 0.82, not 1.00: a goalie who leaks gets pulled before the long tail happens. The price uses a distribution matched to that, and it lands — over 1.5 is priced at 80.0% and went over 79.0%, where a Poisson says 76.5%.
  • His save % barely moves it. Over a season, a goalie's save % is mostly noise around the league's; it's shrunk hard, and it adds almost nothing.
  • Starters are projected, not confirmed. The board prices the goalie with most of his club's recent starts, shows how many, and Results voids a start he didn't make.
  • Built on 10,076 starts across four seasons, fitted walk-forward. The edge here is thin, and the page says so.
